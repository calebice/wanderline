# Watercolor pairing recommendations

## Recommendation

Recommend partners by painting purpose and actual pigment, with the selected wheel hue serving as the entry point. Offer three distinct suggestions: a related color to place beside the selection, a contrasting accent, and a useful paint mixture. A recommendation should explain what to try and what to notice, rather than assign a universal harmony score.

Watercolor requires this distinction because visual complements and mixing complements are different questions. Bruce MacEvoy's tested mixing tables define neutralizing partners at the pigment-and-manufacturer level; they do not support predicting every mixture from geometric opposites on a wheel.[^1] Artist demonstrations also show that near-complements can remain separate for contrast or meet to create useful intermediate tones.[^2]

The catalog below is a starting collection for beginner artwork. It is not a claim that every tube with the same color name behaves identically. No physical swatches were produced to validate these recommendations. Screen colors and gradients are illustrations, not measured paint predictions.

## Evidence and interpretation

**Documented mixture** means a source names the ingredients and describes or demonstrates the result. **Adapted exercise** means the proposed practice applies documented behavior to a selected hue or use case. **Composition suggestion** means an editorial recommendation informed by artists' use of related and contrasting colors; the exact composition has not been experimentally established as superior.

Angela Fehr describes limited palettes organized around related hues or complementary contrast. Her examples include warm gold with turquoise and indigo with a golden yellow.[^3] These support offering purposeful alternatives rather than treating one relationship as best. Thomas Schaller emphasizes value and the painting's idea alongside color, so recommendations should eventually consider light/dark structure and subject, too.[^2]

Confidence belongs to individual claims. A documented mixture can justify “try this for grays” without justifying an exact RGB endpoint, fixed proportion, guaranteed emotional response, or an identical result in another brand. Manufacturer demonstrations are primary evidence for their own products, but commercial sources are not independent comparative trials.

Lime and violet begin with two-pigment preparations, so adding another paint may produce a three-pigment mixture. The app should count and show ingredients accurately rather than call every exercise a two-pigment recipe.

## Exercises to attach to the selections

These exercises are proposed teaching activities, not reproductions of source demonstrations. Work on the same paper intended for the painting. Make a small comparison before committing to a large wash.

1. **Red — Follow the warmth.** Make separate yellow and scarlet pools. Add scarlet gradually to yellow across three swatches. Compare the oranges with an unmixed red accent.
2. **Orange — Choose bright or earthy.** Keep a bright orange swatch separate. In a second study, use Burnt Sienna with Ultramarine and look for a warm gray. Notice that the earth-orange option has a different purpose.
3. **Yellow — Find a leaf color.** Add a small amount of cool blue to cool yellow, then increase the blue in two more swatches. Choose a result that suits the subject rather than automatically choosing the brightest green.
4. **Lime — Turn toward shade.** Begin with yellow-green. Add blue in small increments. Compare the starting mix and the final mix as light and shadow shapes in a leaf.
5. **Green — Find a quieter green.** Introduce crimson gradually. Stop once the green suits the painting; continue separately to discover its darker and redder possibilities.
6. **Mint — Explore dilution.** Make a pale blue-green wash, then add Ultramarine to part of it. Compare the dried mint-like starting wash with the aqua variation.
7. **Cyan — Move toward the sea.** Blend green into Phthalo Blue GS a little at a time. Paint one continuous transition and one pair of separate shapes.
8. **Azure — Try a cloud shadow.** Mix a small amount of Burnt Sienna into Cerulean. Keep an unmixed blue swatch beside it and observe the dried texture and hue.
9. **Blue — Make three grays.** Mix Ultramarine and Burnt Sienna in blue-dominant, approximately balanced, and sienna-dominant pools. Judge balance by the dried result, not equal brushloads.
10. **Violet — Shift a petal shadow.** Start with a Rose/Ultramarine violet. Make a warmer variation with more rose and a cooler one with more blue.
11. **Magenta — Tame the intensity.** Add PG36 sparingly to PR122. Stop at a useful muted color and retain both unmixed endpoints for comparison.
12. **Rose — Preserve a clear petal.** Keep a pale rose passage separate from a rose/ultramarine shadow. Compare placing the colors beside each other with allowing a small boundary to mingle.

For a consistent comparison, use five samples: base alone, base-dominant, an intermediate mixture, partner-dominant, partner alone. These are observational stages, not calibrated 25/50/75 percent paint recipes. Tinting strength and drying behavior make equal quantities an unreliable definition of a neutral mixture.[^1]

## Watercolor behavior the recommendations must respect

**Mixing, mingling and glazing should be separate techniques.** A palette mixture combines paints before application. Wet-on-wet work allows movement on damp paper; wet-on-dry gives more defined edges. Glazing adds another layer over dried color.[^15] The same two names do not fully describe these different experiences.

**Texture is part of a pairing.** The documented Ultramarine/Rose blend combines a settling blue with rose to create a textured purple; a smooth digital gradient cannot represent that separation.[^10] Turquoise made with phthalo ingredients generally differs in texture from versions containing granulating pigments.[^13] Recommend a texture deliberately, and avoid treating granulation as a defect.

**Transparency, staining and granulation are separate properties.** They should not be collapsed into a single “watercolor-like” label. The manufacturer lists Hansa Yellow Medium as semi-transparent, low-staining and non-granulating, while its Pyrrol Scarlet is semi-transparent, medium-staining and non-granulating.[^7][^4] A general claim that every watercolor is transparent would obscure useful product differences.

**An RGB preview is not a mixing model.** Research on semitransparent watercolor prediction uses measured reflectance/transmittance mixture data.[^16] The current arithmetic RGB gradient should remain labeled as a digital transition. Exact-looking pigment recipes require a different evidence base; changing its palette alone cannot provide that.

## Direction for the recommendation feature

Start with a small, curated catalog whose explanations can be audited. Present three choices using language such as **Keep it related**, **Add contrast**, and **Explore a mixture**. Let the selection choose a hue family first, then allow an optional paint identity to refine mixing advice.

Each recommendation should retain: selected hue, example base paint, partner paint or hue, intended use, expected result, technique, short exercise, evidence category, source references, and limitations. A composition suggestion must never acquire a “documented mixture” badge merely because its general color relationship has a citation.

For someone selecting Blue, a useful first presentation is:

> **Burnt Sienna — for grays with warmth**  
> Try it with Ultramarine. Explore a blue-gray, a balanced gray, and a warmer brown. Let the swatches dry before choosing one.[^6]

Offer Rose as a violet-mixing alternative and a restrained earth-orange as a separate composition option. This is an editorial starting order, not a measured ranking of artistic quality.

Later, improve relevance using the paints the artist owns, subject, desired contrast, preferred texture and their saved assessments of dried swatches. A photograph can suggest visible hue families, but cannot establish pigment identity. When only a hue is known, keep mixing advice conditional: “If you are using Ultramarine…”

Before describing results as verified for a product, make physical swatches with recorded brand, pigment codes, paper, dilution and technique. Include dried examples. For recommendations about taste, ask whether the artist found the pair useful rather than claiming objective correctness.

## Footnote presentation

Attach a small source number to the recommendation's explanation. At the bottom of the page, provide an unobtrusive **Sources & pigment notes** disclosure containing the corresponding title and link. No research banner, bibliography cards, or additional research route is needed. Retain full evidence metadata with the catalog so the visible footnote can stay short.

Suggested standing note: “Pairings draw on watercolor demonstrations and pigment references. Digital swatches are illustrative; results vary with paint and technique.”

This report does not change the app or publish a new page. It supplies the evidence and initial recommendation content for a later implementation.
